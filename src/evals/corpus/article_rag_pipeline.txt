Building a Production RAG Pipeline: Lessons From the Trenches

Retrieval-augmented generation has become the default way to ground a language model in your
own data. The idea is simple to state: instead of relying only on what the model memorised
during training, you retrieve relevant passages from a knowledge base at query time and put
them in the prompt. The model then answers using that context. In practice, getting a RAG
system to behave well in production is less about the model and more about everything around
it. This article walks through the pipeline stage by stage and flags the mistakes that cost
us the most time.

Ingestion and chunking

Everything starts with how you split your documents. A model can only use what you retrieve,
and you retrieve chunks, not whole documents. Chunk too large and each chunk mixes several
topics, so a query matches it for the wrong reason and the prompt fills with irrelevant text.
Chunk too small and you sever the context a passage needs to make sense on its own.

After a lot of experimentation we settled on chunks of roughly 500 to 800 tokens with an
overlap of about 10 to 15 percent between neighbours. The overlap matters more than people
expect: without it, a sentence that straddles a chunk boundary gets cut in half and neither
chunk retrieves well for it. We also found that splitting on semantic boundaries such as
headings and paragraphs beats splitting on a fixed character count, because it keeps related
sentences together.

One unglamorous lesson: store metadata with every chunk. The source filename, a section
heading, and a position index are cheap to keep and invaluable later for filtering, for
citations, and for debugging why a particular chunk surfaced.

Embeddings

Each chunk is converted into a dense vector by an embedding model, and the same model embeds
the query at search time. The single most important rule here is consistency: you must embed
your corpus and your queries with the exact same model. Swap the embedding model and forget
to re-index, and retrieval quality collapses silently, because the query vectors now live in
a different space from the stored ones. We learned to treat the embedding model as a versioned
part of the index, not a configuration knob you can change casually.

Retrieval

Dense vector search finds chunks whose embeddings are close to the query embedding, usually by
cosine similarity. It is excellent at matching meaning even when the words differ. Its weakness
is exact terms: product codes, names, and rare jargon that the embedding model never learned to
place precisely. Pure keyword search, such as BM25, has the opposite profile. It nails exact
terms and fails on paraphrase.

The fix that gave us the biggest jump in recall was hybrid search: run both dense and keyword
retrieval, then merge the result lists. A query for an exact error code now finds the right
chunk through the keyword leg even when the dense leg misses it. Combining the two recovered a
class of failures that no amount of embedding tuning had fixed.

Reranking

Retrieval is tuned for recall: cast a wide net and pull, say, the top fifty candidate chunks.
But you only have room for a handful in the prompt, and the order matters because models attend
more to what comes first. This is where a reranker earns its place. A cross-encoder reranker
reads the query and each candidate chunk together and scores their relevance directly, rather
than comparing two independent vectors. It is far more accurate than the first-stage retriever
but too slow to run over the whole corpus, so you apply it only to the shortlist.

Adding a cross-encoder reranker over our top fifty candidates lifted nDCG at ten by roughly
fifteen percent in our offline evaluation. The first-stage retriever still does the heavy
lifting of narrowing millions of chunks down to fifty; the reranker just makes sure the best of
those fifty rise to the top of the prompt.

Generation

With good context in the prompt, generation is the easy part, but two prompt details mattered.
First, instruct the model to answer only from the provided context and to say it does not know
when the context is silent. This single instruction cut hallucinations sharply. Second, ask the
model to cite the chunk it used. Citations make the system auditable and, as a side effect,
nudge the model to stay grounded.

Evaluation

You cannot improve what you do not measure, and RAG fails in more than one place, so measure
each place separately. We score retrieval with recall and nDCG against a labelled set of
questions whose correct documents we know in advance. We score generation with an LLM acting as
a judge that rates two things: faithfulness, meaning whether the answer is supported by the
retrieved context, and answer relevance, meaning whether it actually addresses the question.
Keeping these scores in a regression gate stopped several quiet quality drops from reaching
users after an innocent-looking change to chunking or the prompt.

Common pitfalls

The failures that hurt most were rarely exotic. The embedding model got swapped without a
re-index. Chunks were too large and drowned the prompt in noise. There was no reranker, so a
relevant chunk sat at rank thirty and never made it into the context. And there was no offline
evaluation, so nobody noticed quality slipping until a user complained. None of these is hard to
fix once you can see it. The whole point of building the pipeline deliberately, and measuring
each stage, is to make these failures visible before your users find them for you.
