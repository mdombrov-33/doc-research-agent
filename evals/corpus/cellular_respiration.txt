Cellular respiration is the process cells use to release the energy stored in food and
convert it into a usable form. It is, in a sense, the mirror image of photosynthesis: where
photosynthesis builds glucose and gives off oxygen, respiration breaks glucose down and
consumes oxygen. Most of the action happens inside organelles called mitochondria, often
described as the powerhouses of the cell.

The process unfolds in three main stages. Glycolysis, in the cell's cytoplasm, splits a
molecule of glucose into two molecules of pyruvate and yields a small amount of energy. The
pyruvate then enters the mitochondria, where the citric acid cycle, also called the Krebs
cycle, strips away high-energy electrons and releases carbon dioxide. Finally, those
electrons pass along the electron transport chain, which uses their energy to pump protons
and drive the production of ATP, the molecule that powers almost everything a cell does.

The overall reaction combines glucose and oxygen to produce carbon dioxide, water, and a
large amount of ATP. Because it relies on oxygen, this full pathway is called aerobic
respiration. When oxygen is scarce, cells fall back on fermentation, a much less efficient
route that produces lactic acid or alcohol instead. Together, photosynthesis and respiration
form the energy cycle that sustains nearly all life on Earth.
