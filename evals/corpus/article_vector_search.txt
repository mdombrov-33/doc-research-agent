How Vector Search Works: From Embeddings to Approximate Nearest Neighbours

Vector search is the engine underneath semantic search, recommendation, and retrieval-augmented
generation. The premise is that meaning can be turned into geometry. Once text, images, or audio
are represented as points in a high-dimensional space, "find me things similar to this" becomes
"find the points nearest to this point." This article explains how that search actually works,
from the vectors themselves to the index structures that make search fast at scale.

Embeddings as coordinates

An embedding model maps an input to a vector of a few hundred to a few thousand numbers. The
model is trained so that inputs with similar meaning land close together and dissimilar inputs
land far apart. The individual dimensions rarely have a tidy human interpretation; what matters
is relative position. Two sentences that paraphrase each other end up as neighbours even when
they share no words, which is exactly the property keyword search lacks.

Measuring distance

To find neighbours you need a notion of distance. Three measures dominate. Euclidean distance is
the straight-line gap between two points. Dot product multiplies the vectors component-wise and
sums the result, rewarding vectors that are both aligned and large in magnitude. Cosine
similarity measures only the angle between two vectors, ignoring their length, which is why it is
the usual choice for text: it asks whether two things point in the same direction regardless of
how long the document is. In fact, if you normalise every vector to unit length first, cosine
similarity and dot product rank neighbours identically, which many systems exploit for speed.

The cost of being exact

The obvious way to find the nearest neighbours is brute force: compute the distance from the
query to every stored vector and keep the closest. This is exact and perfectly fine for a few
thousand vectors. It falls apart at scale. Comparing one query against a hundred million vectors,
each with a thousand dimensions, for every search, is far too slow for an interactive system.
This is why production systems trade a little accuracy for a lot of speed and use approximate
nearest neighbour, or ANN, indexes.

HNSW

The most popular ANN index today is the Hierarchical Navigable Small World graph, or HNSW. It
organises vectors into a layered graph. The bottom layer connects every point to its near
neighbours. Each layer above it is a sparser sample of the points below, acting like an express
lane. A search starts at the top, sparse layer and greedily hops toward the query, then drops
down a layer and repeats, refining the neighbourhood at each level until it reaches the bottom.
Because the upper layers let the search cover huge distances in a few hops, it finds good
neighbours after visiting only a tiny fraction of the points. Two parameters govern the trade-off:
ef_construction controls how thoroughly the graph is built, and ef_search controls how widely the
query explores at query time. Larger values mean better recall and slower search.

IVF

An alternative family is the inverted file index, or IVF. It first clusters all the vectors into
groups using something like k-means, picking a representative centroid for each group. At search
time, instead of scanning everything, you find the few centroids nearest the query and search only
the vectors in those clusters. You tune how many clusters to probe: probe more and you raise recall
at the cost of speed. IVF shines when you have a very large, fairly static dataset.

Product quantization

Memory is the other constraint, and this is where product quantization comes in. Storing a hundred
million full-precision vectors can demand hundreds of gigabytes of RAM. Product quantization splits
each vector into several short sub-vectors and replaces each sub-vector with the id of the nearest
entry in a small learned codebook. A vector that once took thousands of bytes now takes a few dozen.
The trade-off is precision: the compressed vector is an approximation, so distances computed from it
are slightly off. In exchange you fit far more vectors in memory, and quantization is often layered
on top of an IVF or HNSW index to get both speed and a small footprint at once.

Choosing an index

There is no universally best index, only the right trade-off for your constraints. If your dataset
is small, brute force is simplest and exact. If you need high recall and your data fits in memory,
HNSW is the usual default. If memory is tight, add product quantization and accept a little accuracy
loss. If your data is enormous and changes rarely, IVF with quantization scales gracefully. The
common thread is that vector search at scale is the art of giving up a sliver of accuracy you will
never notice in exchange for speed and memory you definitely will.
