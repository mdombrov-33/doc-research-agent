The User Datagram Protocol, or UDP, is one of the core transport-layer protocols of the
internet, sitting alongside TCP. Unlike TCP, UDP is connectionless: it sends independent
packets called datagrams without first establishing a connection and without any handshake.
There is no built-in mechanism to confirm that a datagram arrived, to retransmit lost data,
or to put packets back into order.

This stripped-down design is a deliberate trade-off. Because UDP skips connection setup,
acknowledgments, and retransmission, it has very low overhead and latency. That makes it the
right choice for applications where speed matters more than perfect reliability, and where a
late packet is worse than a missing one. Live video and voice calls, online games, and DNS
lookups all commonly run over UDP; a momentary dropped frame in a video call is preferable to
the stall that waiting for a retransmission would cause.

Applications that use UDP but still need some reliability must build it themselves on top of
the protocol. UDP simply provides ports, so that multiple programs on the same machine can
send and receive datagrams, and a lightweight checksum to detect corruption. Everything
else is left to the application. In short, TCP favors reliable, ordered delivery, while UDP
favors low latency and simplicity.
