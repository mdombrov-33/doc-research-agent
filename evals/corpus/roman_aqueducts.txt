Roman aqueducts were among the greatest engineering achievements of the ancient world.
They carried fresh water from distant springs and rivers into cities, supplying public
fountains, baths, and private homes. The city of Rome alone was served by eleven major
aqueducts, some drawing water from more than fifty kilometers away.

The aqueducts worked entirely by gravity. Engineers surveyed a route with a very gentle
and continuous downward gradient, often only a few centimeters of drop per hundred meters,
so that water flowed steadily without pooling or running too fast. Most of the channel ran
underground to protect the water and follow the terrain, but where the land dipped into a
valley the Romans raised the channel on tiers of stone arches to keep the gradient constant.

To cross especially deep valleys, builders sometimes used an inverted siphon: the water
descended in sealed lead or stone pipes, gained pressure at the bottom, and rose again on
the far side. Settling tanks along the route let sediment drop out, and distribution tanks
called castella divided the flow among different users. Careful maintenance crews cleared
mineral deposits and repaired leaks, allowing some aqueducts to remain in service for
centuries.
