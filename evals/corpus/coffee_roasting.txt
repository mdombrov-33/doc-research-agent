Coffee roasting is the process of applying heat to green coffee beans to develop the flavors
and aromas we associate with coffee. Raw green beans are dense, grassy, and almost flavorless;
roasting drives off moisture and triggers chemical reactions that transform them into the
fragrant brown beans used for brewing.

As the beans heat up, they pass through recognizable stages. They first turn yellow and give
off a smell like toast or hay as moisture leaves. Around two hundred degrees Celsius the beans
audibly crack, an event roasters call first crack, caused by steam and gases breaking out of
the bean. The browning is driven largely by the Maillard reaction and by caramelization of
sugars, which together create hundreds of aromatic compounds. A lighter roast, stopped soon
after first crack, keeps more of the bean's original acidity and fruity character. Pushed
further, into a second crack, the beans grow darker, oilier, and more bitter, with roast
flavors overtaking the bean's origin notes.

The roaster controls the outcome by managing time and temperature, charting a roast curve
that balances development against scorching. Freshly roasted beans then need a day or two to
rest and release carbon dioxide before they brew at their best. Roast level, bean origin, and
freshness together set the stage for everything that happens later in the cup.
