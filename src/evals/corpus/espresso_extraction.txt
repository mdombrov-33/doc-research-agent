Espresso is brewed by forcing hot water under high pressure through a compact puck of finely
ground coffee. A typical shot uses around nine bars of pressure and water close to ninety-
three degrees Celsius, pushed through the grounds for roughly twenty-five to thirty seconds.
The result is a concentrated drink topped with a layer of reddish-brown foam called crema.

Extraction quality depends on several variables working together. The grind size controls
how quickly water passes through the puck: too coarse and the water rushes through, under-
extracting and leaving a sour, weak shot; too fine and the water struggles through, over-
extracting and producing a bitter, harsh cup. The dose of coffee, how evenly it is
distributed, and how firmly it is tamped all affect resistance and flow as well.

Baristas judge a shot by its taste, its color, and how long it takes to pour. They adjust
the grind first, since it has the largest effect, and then fine-tune the dose and water
temperature. Fresh beans matter too, because coffee releases carbon dioxide for days after
roasting, and that gas influences how the water saturates the grounds. Balancing all of
these factors yields a sweet, syrupy shot with a lasting crema.
