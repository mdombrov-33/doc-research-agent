Plate tectonics is the scientific theory that explains how Earth's outer shell is divided
into large moving pieces called plates. This rigid outer layer, the lithosphere, is broken
into about a dozen major plates and several smaller ones that slowly drift over the hotter,
softer rock beneath them. They move only a few centimeters a year, roughly the rate at which
fingernails grow, but over millions of years that motion reshapes the surface of the planet.

Most geological action happens at the boundaries where plates meet, and there are three main
kinds. At divergent boundaries plates pull apart, and molten rock rises to fill the gap,
creating new crust along mid-ocean ridges. At convergent boundaries plates collide; the
denser one may dive beneath the other in a process called subduction, building mountain
ranges and deep ocean trenches and feeding volcanoes. At transform boundaries plates grind
past one another sideways, storing stress that is released as earthquakes.

The driving force comes from heat deep inside the Earth, which sets up slow churning currents
in the mantle that drag the plates along. Plate tectonics ties together once-separate puzzles:
the matching coastlines of distant continents, the distribution of earthquakes and volcanoes,
and the slow drift of the continents across geologic time.
