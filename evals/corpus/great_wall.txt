The Great Wall of China is a vast system of fortifications built and rebuilt over many
centuries along the historical northern borders of China. Rather than a single continuous
structure, it is a network of walls, watchtowers, and garrisons stretching for thousands of
kilometers across mountains, deserts, and grasslands. Its main purpose was defensive: to
slow and channel raids by nomadic peoples from the north and to control movement and trade.

Construction spanned many dynasties. Early states built rammed-earth walls as far back as
the seventh century BCE, and the first emperor of a unified China linked several of them
together around 220 BCE. The most famous and best-preserved sections, however, were built
much later by the Ming dynasty, beginning in the fourteenth century. Ming engineers faced
stone and brick over an earthen core, added crenellated battlements, and spaced signal
towers within sight of one another so that warnings could be relayed quickly using smoke by
day and fire by night.

The wall doubled as a road for moving troops and supplies along the frontier, and its gates
served as checkpoints for taxing caravans. While it never made the border impenetrable, it
was an effective tool for surveillance and slowing attackers. Today its surviving stretches
are among the most recognizable monuments in the world.
