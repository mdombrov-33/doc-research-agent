Chapter Seven: A Global History of Coffee

Few commodities have travelled as far, or stirred as much suspicion and delight, as the roasted
seed of a small evergreen shrub. Coffee is today so ordinary that we forget it was once exotic,
contraband, sacred, and feared in turn. To trace its journey from a wild plant in the Ethiopian
highlands to the cup on a commuter's desk is to follow a thread through nine centuries of religion,
trade, empire, and revolution. This chapter follows that thread.

Origins in the Ethiopian highlands

The coffee plant is native to the forests of the Ethiopian highlands, where the species Coffea
arabica still grows wild. Long before anyone roasted or brewed it, the peoples of the region are
thought to have eaten the energising cherries, sometimes crushed with fat into a kind of portable
ration. The plant's stimulating effect was the discovery; the drink we recognise came much later.

The most enduring origin story is almost certainly a legend, but it has been told so often that it
deserves its place. According to the tale, a goatherd named Kaldi noticed that his goats became
unusually frisky, even dancing, after nibbling the bright red cherries of a certain shrub. Curious,
Kaldi tried the cherries himself and felt the same restless energy. He is said to have brought them
to a local monastery, where a monk disapproved and threw them into the fire, only for the roasting
aroma to draw everyone back. Whatever the truth of Kaldi and his dancing goats, the story captures
something real: coffee entered human culture through its effect on the body before it became a
matter of taste.

Yemen and the first true coffee culture

Coffee as a brewed drink first took recognisable shape across the Red Sea, in Yemen, by the
fifteenth century. It was there, in the Sufi monasteries, that coffee found its first devoted
community. The monks drank it to stay awake and alert through long nights of prayer and devotion,
and this religious use spread the habit through the wider population. The Arabic word qahwa, which
had once described a kind of wine, was attached to the new beverage.

Yemen also gave coffee its first commercial hub. The port of Mocha, on the Red Sea coast, became so
central to the trade that its name still clings to coffee centuries later. For a long time Yemen
guarded its monopoly fiercely. Beans intended for export were treated, often by parboiling or
drying, so that they could not be planted and germinated elsewhere. Coffee could leave Mocha as a
drink in waiting, but not as a living plant. This jealous control held, more or less, for
generations, and it shaped everything about how coffee would eventually escape.

The coffee house is born

From Yemen the drink spread north through the Islamic world, and with it came an entirely new kind of
public space. The qahveh khaneh, or coffee house, appeared in cities such as Mecca, Cairo, and above
all Constantinople, where the first coffee houses are commonly dated to around 1554. These were
unlike anything that had come before. Taverns served alcohol and the disreputable; the coffee house
served a stimulant that sharpened the mind, and it welcomed conversation, chess, music, poetry, and
the trading of news.

That very liveliness made coffee houses suspect to authorities. A place where men of all sorts
gathered to talk freely, fuelled by a drink that kept them awake and sharp, looked to nervous rulers
like a breeding ground for sedition. Coffee was banned more than once, in Mecca in the early
sixteenth century and periodically in Constantinople, sometimes on religious grounds and sometimes
on frankly political ones. The bans never held. The institution was too useful and too pleasurable,
and each prohibition gave way to a return.

Coffee reaches Europe

Coffee arrived in Europe through the great trading city of Venice, whose merchants did business
across the eastern Mediterranean. By around 1615 the drink was being sold in Venice, at first by
lemonade vendors and as a costly novelty. As with everything new and foreign, it met resistance.
A famous, possibly embellished, episode has advisers urging Pope Clement VIII to condemn coffee as
the drink of the Muslim infidel. The Pope, the story goes, tasted it first and found it so
delicious that he joked it should be baptised to make it properly Christian. True or not, the tale
marks the moment coffee was accepted rather than shunned in Catholic Europe.

From Venice the drink spread north and west, and the coffee house came with it. England took to the
institution with particular enthusiasm. The first English coffee house opened in Oxford in 1650,
serving the university crowd, and London followed in 1652, when a man named Pasqua Rosée opened a
premises in St Michael's Alley in the City. London's coffee houses multiplied with astonishing speed.

The penny universities and the birth of an insurance market

English coffee houses earned a nickname that captures their character perfectly: the penny
universities. For the price of a cup, a penny, a man could sit for hours among merchants, scholars,
writers, and politicians, and learn more from the conversation than many learned at a real
university. Each house developed its own clientele. Some drew poets and wits, others men of science,
others traders in particular goods. The coffee house became, in effect, a specialised office and
news exchange before such things had formal buildings of their own.

The most consequential of these specialisations unfolded near the river. In 1686 a man named Edward
Lloyd opened a coffee house on Tower Street, later moving to Lombard Street, that became the
favourite haunt of ship owners, captains, and merchants with cargoes at sea. Lloyd recognised what
his customers wanted and began compiling and circulating reliable shipping news, lists of vessels
and their movements and conditions. Men who wished to insure ships and cargo against the perils of
the voyage met there to strike their bargains. Out of that informal marketplace, conducted over
coffee, grew Lloyd's of London, which became the most famous insurance market in the world. An
institution that still underwrites global risk today began as a coffee house, and it kept the name
of the man who poured the coffee.

Coffee houses played a similar incubating role elsewhere in finance and commerce. London's stock
dealers, expelled from the Royal Exchange for their rowdiness, conducted business in nearby coffee
houses such as Jonathan's and Garraway's, and these gatherings fed into what would become the London
Stock Exchange. The pattern repeats: a stimulating drink, a public room, free conversation, and out
of it institutions that reshaped economic life.

Breaking the monopoly: coffee escapes its homeland

For all its spread as a drink, coffee the plant remained, for a long time, a closely held secret of
Yemen and the lands around it. The story of how the living plant escaped is a story of smuggling,
diplomacy, and a few audacious individuals.

The Dutch were among the first to break the hold. Through a combination of trade and quiet theft,
they obtained live coffee plants and, by the late seventeenth century, were cultivating coffee in
their colonial holdings, most famously on the island of Java in the East Indies. Java grew so
productive that its name, like Mocha's, became a byword for coffee itself. The Dutch also kept plants
in the botanical garden at Amsterdam, and one of those plants would seed coffee's leap across the
Atlantic.

That leap is bound up with a French naval officer named Gabriel de Clieu. Around 1723 he obtained a
seedling descended from the Amsterdam stock and carried it by ship to the Caribbean island of
Martinique. The voyage became legendary: water ran short, and de Clieu is said to have shared his own
meagre ration with the fragile plant to keep it alive through the crossing. The seedling survived,
took root in Martinique, and from that single plant, by most accounts, descended a great many of the
coffee trees that would later cover the Caribbean and Central America.

The most important transfer of all carried coffee to Brazil, the country that would come to dominate
world production. Here too the tale turns on cunning. In 1727 a Brazilian official named Francisco de
Melo Palheta was sent to French Guiana, ostensibly to mediate a border dispute, but with the quiet
aim of acquiring coffee plants the French were determined not to share. According to the story,
Palheta charmed the governor's wife, who at a farewell dinner presented him with a bouquet in which
coffee seeds and cuttings had been hidden. Whether by seduction or simple diplomacy, Palheta returned
with the means to start Brazil's coffee industry. The climate proved ideal, and within a century
Brazil was producing more coffee than the rest of the world combined, transforming the global market
and, less happily, driving the expansion of slave labour on its plantations.

Industrialisation and the modern cup

As coffee became a mass commodity, technology reshaped how it was prepared. The nineteenth and early
twentieth centuries brought a wave of invention aimed at making coffee faster, more consistent, and
more portable. Vacuum packing kept roasted coffee fresh far from where it was grown. Soluble or
instant coffee, perfected and mass-produced in the early twentieth century, turned the drink into a
matter of hot water and a spoon.

The most romantic of these inventions was the espresso machine, born of an Italian impatience with
waiting. In 1901 an engineer named Luigi Bezzera patented a machine that forced steam and water
through finely ground coffee under pressure, producing a small, intense cup quickly and on demand. The
design was commercialised by Desiderio Pavoni and refined over the following decades. A further leap
came in 1948, when Achille Gaggia introduced a lever-driven machine that pushed water through the
grounds at far higher pressure, producing the layer of golden crema that defines modern espresso. The
café culture that espresso enabled spread from Italy across the world in the second half of the
twentieth century.

Conclusion

The seed that Kaldi's goats supposedly discovered has, in the centuries since, financed insurance
markets, filled the rooms where revolutions were argued, crossed oceans hidden in bouquets and shared
with a sailor's water ration, and ended up dispensed by machines that hiss in every city on earth.
Coffee's history is not really the history of a drink. It is the history of the places people gathered
to drink it, and of the lengths to which they would go to get it.
