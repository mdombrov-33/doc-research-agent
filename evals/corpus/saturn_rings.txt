Saturn is the sixth planet from the Sun and the second largest in the solar system, but it is
best known for its spectacular ring system, the most extensive and visible of any planet.
Through even a small telescope the rings are an unmistakable sight, circling the planet's
equator in a broad, bright disk.

Despite looking solid from a distance, the rings are made of countless individual particles,
ranging from tiny grains of dust to chunks of ice and rock several meters across. They are
overwhelmingly water ice, which is why they reflect sunlight so brightly. The rings are
enormous in width, spanning hundreds of thousands of kilometers, yet astonishingly thin, in
many places only tens of meters thick. They are organized into broad bands labeled with
letters in the order of their discovery, separated by gaps such as the Cassini Division.

Astronomers still debate how the rings formed. Leading ideas suggest they are the shattered
remains of a moon or comet torn apart by Saturn's gravity, or leftover material that never
coalesced into a moon. Saturn's many moons help shape the rings: small shepherd moons orbit
within and beside them, their gravity keeping ring edges sharp and carving out the gaps.
Evidence suggests the rings may be relatively young and are slowly raining down onto the
planet.
